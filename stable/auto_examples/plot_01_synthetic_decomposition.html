A series with a known decomposition#

The model is handed a series built from the parts it is meant to find: a linear trend, a slow oscillation whose frequency rises through the sample and whose amplitude steps up two-thirds of the way through, a faster oscillation at a fixed frequency, and noise. The example fits the default filtered alignment, reads every path off the result, checks the identity that ties them together, and then asks the only question that matters about a decomposition with a known answer: how did the network divide the series between its trend head and its components?

The answer, on a short schedule, is the behaviour the user guide’s nuances page describes: the trend head reconstructs the series and the components carry little. The example is therefore less a demonstration that the model recovers planted sinusoids than a reading of what it does instead, with the tools that make that reading quick.

The series#

Four hundred and eighty steps. The slow component’s frequency runs from 0.08 to 0.20 radians per step (periods of 79 down to 31 steps), its amplitude from 0.5 to 1.5 around step 300; the fast component sits at 0.60 (a period of about 10 steps).

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

from ns_sdn.spectral_decomposition import NSSDN, Stage

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 480
t = np.arange(n, dtype=float)
omega_slow = 0.08 + 0.00025 * t
amp_slow = 1.0 + 0.5 * np.tanh((t - 300) / 30)
slow = amp_slow * np.sin(np.cumsum(omega_slow))
fast = 0.4 * np.sin(0.60 * t)
trend_true = 0.004 * t
y = trend_true + slow + fast + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(n)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 3.5))
ax.plot(t, y, lw=0.9, label="series")
ax.plot(t, trend_true, lw=1.2, ls="--", label="planted trend")
ax.set_xlabel("step")
ax.legend(loc="upper left")
ax.set_title("A trend, a chirp with a step in amplitude, a fast sinusoid, noise")
plt.tight_layout()

Fit#

Three components, a 32-dimensional state and one stage of three hundred Adam steps: a fraction of the published configuration, chosen so the page builds in under a minute. seed fixes the initialization.

res = NSSDN(y, k=3, state_dim=32, schedule=(Stage(5e-3, 300),), seed=0).fit()
print(res.summary())

The identity#

Trend plus the sum of the component contributions is the emission, exactly, and the emission reconstructs the series closely: under the filtered alignment the state at t has seen y[t].

rebuilt = res.trend + res.components.sum(axis=1)
print("identity holds:", bool(np.allclose(rebuilt, res.emission, atol=1e-5)))
print(f"emission RMSE: {np.sqrt(np.mean(res.reconstruction_resid**2)):.4f}")
print(f"one-step RMSE: {np.sqrt(res.sigma2):.4f}")

identity holds: True
emission RMSE: 0.0180
one-step RMSE: 0.2187

Where the variance went#

The question with a known answer. The planted oscillations account for most of the series’ variance; the decomposition’s oscillation is what the components account for.

parts = {
    "series": y,
    "planted oscillation": slow + fast,
    "fitted trend": res.trend,
    "fitted oscillation": res.oscillation,
}
for name, values in parts.items():
    print(f"{name:>22s} variance {np.var(values):8.4f}")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10, 6), sharex=True)
axes[0].plot(t, y, lw=0.8, label="series")
axes[0].plot(t, res.emission, lw=0.8, label="emission")
axes[0].plot(t, res.trend, lw=1.4, label="trend head $B_t$")
axes[0].legend(loc="upper left")
axes[0].set_title("The trend head tracks the series; the emission is trend plus components")
axes[1].plot(t, slow + fast, lw=0.8, label="planted oscillation")
axes[1].plot(t, res.oscillation, lw=1.2, label="components' contribution")

axes[1].set_xlabel("step")

The spectral state#

The same reading in permutation-invariant coordinates: the centroid and dispersion of the power-weighted frequency measure, and how many components it effectively uses.

The predicted alignment#

Trained to predict y[t] from the state after y[t - 1] instead, the network is a forecaster and the one-step error is the thing it minimizes. The random walk is the benchmark: it predicts each value by the one before it, which on a smooth series is already most of the way.

fwd = NSSDN(y, k=3, state_dim=32, alignment="predicted", schedule=(Stage(5e-3, 300),), seed=0).fit()
naive = y[:-1]
target = y[1:]
rmse = lambda a, b: float(np.sqrt(np.mean((a - b) ** 2)))  # noqa: E731
print(f"one-step RMSE, filtered:  {rmse(target, res.fittedvalues):.4f}")
print(f"one-step RMSE, predicted: {rmse(target, fwd.fittedvalues):.4f}")
print(f"one-step RMSE, random walk: {rmse(target, naive):.4f}")
print(f"predicted alignment, fitted oscillation variance: {np.var(fwd.oscillation):.4f}")

one-step RMSE, filtered:  0.2187
one-step RMSE, predicted: 0.0864
one-step RMSE, random walk: 0.2166
predicted alignment, fitted oscillation variance: 0.0004

What this shows#

On this schedule the trend head has done the work under both alignments: it reconstructs the series under the filtered one and reproduces the previous value under the predicted one, while the components’ share of the variance stays near zero. One component’s frequency does settle close to the planted 0.60, which is also near the softplus initialization, but its gated amplitude is a few hundredths; nothing finds the 0.08-to-0.20 chirp, whose frequency the drift penalty would have to be pulled a long way to reach. The paths are smooth and the identities hold, which is what the package guarantees; whether the components carry the oscillation is a property of the fit, not of the API, and the tools above are how to check it. The published DGS10 run, with sixteen components and three thousand steps, is the configuration the papers read structure from; the next example runs a shorter version of it.
