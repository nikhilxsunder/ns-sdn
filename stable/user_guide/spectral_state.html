The spectral state#

Sixteen amplitude paths and sixteen frequency paths are a lot to read, and a claim such as “the spectrum compressed in 2020” is a claim about all of them at once. SpectralState reduces the decomposition at each step to a probability measure on the frequency axis and reports the coordinates of that measure: where it sits, how spread it is, how many components it effectively uses, how fast it moves, and how well the components can be told apart. The coordinates are diagnostics of a model trained on the prediction criterion alone; nothing here enters a loss.

The measure#

At row \(t\) component \(k\) contributes with amplitude \(a_{t,k} = |w_k|\, g_{t,k} A_{t,k}\) (effective_amplitude) at frequency \(\omega_{t,k}\). Its power is \(p_{t,k} = a_{t,k}^2\), and normalizing gives a discrete measure on the frequency axis,

\[\mu_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_{t,k}\, \delta_{\omega_{t,k}}, \qquad \pi_{t,k} = \frac{p_{t,k}}{\sum_j p_{t,j}}.\]

weight is \(\pi\), shape (r, k); frequency is the support. Every coordinate below is a functional of \(\mu_t\).

Position and spread#

\[\bar\omega_t = \sum_k \pi_{t,k}\, \omega_{t,k}, \qquad s_t = \Bigl(\sum_k \pi_{t,k} (\omega_{t,k} - \bar\omega_t)^2\Bigr)^{1/2}.\]

centroid is the power-weighted mean frequency, dispersion the power-weighted standard deviation. A falling centroid is the spectrum slowing down; a falling dispersion is the components converging in frequency.

Effective count#

\[H_t = -\sum_k \pi_{t,k} \log \pi_{t,k}, \qquad m_t = e^{H_t}, \qquad \mathrm{IPR}_t = \Bigl(\sum_k \pi_{t,k}^2\Bigr)^{-1}.\]

entropy is the spectral entropy, effective_count its exponential (between 1 and \(K\): the number of equally weighted components that would have the same entropy), and participation_ratio the inverse participation ratio, a second effective count that weights the large components more. Both fall when power concentrates in fewer components.

Compression, in these terms, is a joint contraction of dispersion and effective_count: the components move together in frequency and fewer of them carry the power. Either alone is a different story, and the IEEE-ORSS reading of 2020, frequency convergence with amplitude concentration, is the first.

Velocity#

How fast the measure moves between consecutive rows is the 1-Wasserstein distance [Villani, 2009] per unit time,

\[\mathcal{V}_t = \frac{W_1(\mu_t, \mu_{t-1})}{\Delta t_t}, \qquad W_1(\mu, \nu) = \int \bigl|F_\mu(\omega) - F_\nu(\omega)\bigr|\, d\omega,\]

computed in closed form from the two cumulative distribution functions. velocity has one entry fewer than the rows, since it sits between them. Two decompositions of the same step distinguish why the measure moved: velocity_frequency is \(W_1\) between the measures at \(t\) and \(t - 1\) with the weights frozen at \(t - 1\) (the components moved), and velocity_mass with the frequencies frozen at \(t - 1\) (the power moved between components). They do not add exactly to velocity, but their relative size says whether a spike in it was a shift in frequencies or a reallocation of power.

Active set and disentanglement#

A component whose weight is tiny is unidentified: its frequency, phase and offset are not pinned down by the data because it contributes nothing to the emission. active marks, per row and component, whether \(\pi_{t,k}\) is at least active_threshold (default 0.01), and per-component readings should be restricted to active rows.

contribution_correlation is the \(K \times K\) correlation matrix of the components paths over the sample, and disentanglement_score is one minus the off-diagonal share of its squared entries, in \([0, 1]\): one when the contributions are mutually uncorrelated, falling as components come to carry the same movement. The measure alone cannot tell converged frequencies from redundant components, and this score is the check: identifiability degrades exactly as \(\omega_k \to \omega_l\), so the compression regime is also the regime in which per-component readings are least trustworthy. State that caveat with any compression claim rather than beside it.

Computing it#

spectral_state() on a result, or SpectralState.from_result:

import numpy as np
from ns_sdn.spectral_decomposition import NSSDN, Stage

t = np.arange(240.0)
y = np.sin(0.3 * t) + 0.5 * np.sin(0.07 * t)
res = NSSDN(y, k=3, state_dim=8, schedule=(Stage(1e-2, 40),), seed=0).fit()
state = res.spectral_state()

print(state.nobs, state.k)
print(state.weight.shape, state.centroid.shape, state.velocity.shape, state.active.shape)
print(bool(np.allclose(state.weight.sum(axis=1), 1.0)))
print(bool(np.all(state.effective_count >= 1.0)), bool(np.all(state.effective_count <= 3.0)))
print(0.0 <= state.disentanglement_score <= 1.0)

summary() prints the min, mean and max of each scalar coordinate with the disentanglement score and the mean number of active components in the header; to_dict() and to_pandas(index=...) lay the scalar coordinates out one row per path row, velocities padded with nan in the first row so the frames align:

table = state.to_dict()
print(sorted(table))
print(np.isnan(table["velocity"][0]), table["velocity"].shape)

Testing a regime claim#

A regime-change claim in these coordinates is a before-and-after comparison. Mark the event on the result’s time, and compare the coordinates over a window on each side:

event = 160
before = slice(event - 40, event)
after = slice(event, event + 40)
for name in ("centroid", "dispersion", "effective_count"):
    values = getattr(state, name)
    print(name, values[before].shape, values[after].shape)

centroid (40,) (40,)
dispersion (40,) (40,)
effective_count (40,) (40,)

What makes the comparison evidence is that it holds across refits and across k: the coordinates are invariant to the permutation of components, which the raw paths are not, so a seed sweep over the same specification is the natural check. The DGS10 example draws the coordinates with the 2020 window marked.
