Forecasting#

The model produces two kinds of forecast and trains under two alignments, and the four combinations do not mean the same thing. This page states each protocol as the package implements it, says which one the published numbers are, and gives the benchmark every one-step number should be read against.

One-step conditional#

The published evaluation runs the trained network over the training and holdout observations together under teacher forcing, every input a realized value, and reads the emission at each step as the prediction of the next. predict is that protocol: for a series of length \(m\) it returns \(m - 1\) values aligned with endog[1:], each built from realized values through the step before. The published holdout numbers are the last len(holdout) of them:

import numpy as np
from ns_sdn.spectral_decomposition import NSSDN, Stage

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
t = np.arange(260.0)
y = 0.002 * t + np.sin(0.2 * t) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(260)
train, holdout = y[:230], y[230:]

res = NSSDN(train, k=2, state_dim=8, schedule=(Stage(1e-2, 40),), seed=0).fit()
one_step = res.predict(y)[-len(holdout):]
print(one_step.shape)

(30,)

On the fitted sample itself predict reproduces fittedvalues, so the in-sample one-step statistics on the result (resid, sigma2, llf, r_squared) are the same protocol applied to the training segment:

print(bool(np.allclose(res.predict(train), res.fittedvalues)))
print(bool(np.allclose(res.resid, train[1:] - res.fittedvalues)))

True
True

The random walk#

A one-step forecast of a persistent series looks good by construction: predicting each value by the one before it is already hard to beat, and the network conditions on that value. The first question about any one-step number is therefore how it compares with the no-change forecast, and the summary’s first note says so. Compute both:

def rmse(actual, predicted):
    return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((predicted - actual) ** 2)))

naive = y[len(train) - 1 : -1]              # y[t-1] as the forecast of y[t]
print(round(rmse(holdout, one_step), 2), round(rmse(holdout, naive), 2))

0.2... 0.2...

On this short fit the random walk wins outright; on the published DGS10 run the one-step errors are of the same order as the daily changes of the yield. The reproduction example adds the random-walk row to the paper’s metrics table for exactly this reason. Skill over persistence is a claim to establish, not a property of the protocol.

Recursive#

forecast(steps) warms the network up on the fitted sample under teacher forcing, feeds the last observation as the first future input, and then feeds each emission back as the next input. No realized value enters after the first step, so error compounds with the horizon; the validation notebook behind the papers found this mode far weaker than the conditional one, and the IEEE-ORSS paper evaluates the conditional protocol for that reason.

path = res.forecast(12)
print(path.shape, bool(np.all(np.isfinite(path))))

(12,) True

A fit with covariates needs exog of shape (steps, p) here, and an irregular grid continues through time, a (steps,) array of future times after the sample’s last; without it the sample’s last step size is repeated.

Filtered and predicted#

The alignment decides what the emission at \(t\) has seen, and therefore what each protocol measures.

alignment="filtered" (default, published)

alignment="predicted"

Under the filtered alignment the network is trained to reproduce the observation it has just seen, so emission fits the series closely (the Emission RMSE of the summary) while fittedvalues carries the same emission forward by one step and is judged against the next observation (the One-step RMSE). The gap between the two RMSEs is the one-step change in the series the decomposition did not anticipate. Under the predicted alignment the two coincide, because the emission is the one-step prediction, and the network trains as a forecaster:

fwd = NSSDN(train, k=2, state_dim=8, alignment="predicted",
            schedule=(Stage(1e-2, 40),), seed=0).fit()
print(fwd.emission.shape, fwd.fittedvalues.shape, fwd.observed.shape)
print(bool(np.allclose(fwd.emission, fwd.fittedvalues)))

(229,) (229,) (229,)
True

Which to use. The filtered alignment is the published model and the right one for reading the decomposition of an observed series, which is what the IEEE-ORSS paper does; its one-step numbers are a by-product and should be benchmarked. The predicted alignment is the right one when the forecast is the object, with the caveat on the nuances page that a GRU with a linear trend head trained to predict a persistent series learns persistence first and oscillation later, if at all, under short schedules.

Fit statistics#

The one-step residuals carry the fit statistics, under both alignments:

sigma2

Mean squared one-step residual; its root is the summary’s One-step RMSE.

llf

The concentrated Gaussian log-likelihood \(-\tfrac{n}{2}\bigl(\log 2\pi\hat\sigma^2 + 1\bigr)\) of the one-step residuals, with \(n\) the effective sample.

r_squared

One minus the residual share of the variance of endog[1:].

information_criteria, aic, bic, hqic

From llf, nobs and n_params.

compare(*others, criterion=...) ranks results fitted to the same series. Read it with the summary’s second note in mind: the likelihood is concentrated at a penalized fit and n_params is the nominal weight count, which overstates the freedom of a weight-decayed network, so the criteria discriminate between values of k within one engine configuration and not across configurations. The metrics of the papers (RMSE, MAE, MAPE on the holdout, on the scaled, log and level series) are computed from predict by the reproduction example.
